# WHY MICROGAMES, WHY NOW?
## Office Attack | CCDP10003 Project 2 research integrated into the playable intermission

**9 November 2026.** Evidence-led critical context. The interactive game credits are optional and contain three browsable panels, not a mandatory essay.

### Central question
**Can a tiny, funny, finite game offer meaningful active participation during a spare moment, rather than simply filling that moment with another endless feed?**

The proposal is about *interaction design and player agency*. It does not promise therapeutic effects, reduced smartphone dependency or a measured recovery in attention span.

### 1. Origin: short and expressive games predate short-form feeds
Nintendo describes *WarioWare: Move It!* (2023) as more than 200 lightning-fast microgames, each lasting seconds. The series demonstrates an instantly readable imperative, one dominant action, surprise, quick success/failure and escalating pressure. *WarioWare: Get It Together!* (2021) retains short-form microgame play with a handheld-compatible Switch mode.

Melbourne's *Dumb Ways to Die* began as Metro Trains' rail-safety campaign and its 2013 game adapted absurd survival situations into rapid interactive challenges. Games for Change documents tap, tilt and gesture tasks, three lives and increasing speed. The official game site lists 82 minigames in its current edition. The comparison is mechanical and critical, **not** a claim to have copied Nintendo or Dumb Ways characters, artwork or audiovisual assets.

**Design consequence:** Office Attack uses six one-command tasks, real input, quick comedy feedback and two boss encounters. Every action has a clear consequence and short deadline. The visual theme is original office-disaster comedy.

### 2. The pocket-play opportunity
Keogh and Richardson explain that short and irregular mobile sessions can fit around waiting, commuting or other daily activities; their 2017/2018 research complicates a simplistic split between 'casual' and 'serious' players. The 2025 ESA survey says 68% of adult American players list passing time or relaxation as a motivation. WarioWare's Nintendo Switch catalogue demonstrates portable access (the 2021 title supports handheld mode; the 2023 motion-controlled *Move It!* explicitly does not).

**What is supported:** Flexible short-session play exists, and established microgame games continue to be produced. **What is not supported:** A measured commercial 'comeback' in the entire microgame genre or proof that newer handheld demand is caused by shrinking attention spans.

**Design consequence:** A browser-based, mouse/touch-friendly arcade that can be completed in a spare break, stopped immediately after failure, and resumed only by clicking Continue at its rest stops.

Sources:
- Brendan Keogh & Ingrid Richardson, [*Waiting to play: The labour of background games*](https://doi.org/10.1177/1367549417705603), *European Journal of Cultural Studies* 21(1), 13–25; first published 2017.

### 3. Attention, scroll culture, and what we must *not* claim
A 2026 systematic review of 17 studies on young adults found preliminary associations between heavier short-form video usage, attention-related difficulties and compulsive engagement. It also states that the evidence is heterogeneous, often cross-sectional/self-reported, and **cannot establish causality or direction**. A separate synthesis of 71 studies found correlated cognition measures, not proof that attention spans are irreversibly shrinking.

Terms like 'dopamine junkies' can be an expressive brainstorm but are not an accurate scientific description of audiences or evidence for this design. Neither review establishes that gaming is intrinsically better than scrolling or that an arcade game is an effective remedy.

**Design hypothesis:** The user actively solves a bounded, tactile challenge, gets an immediate outcome and reaches a finite ending, instead of passively consuming an auto-advancing feed. Importantly, the game's pressure deliberately stops during the two intermissions. This makes control of the player's attention part of the artwork itself.

Sources:
- [Arouch et al., *Short-form video, cognitive and mental health outcomes: a systematic review*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42781066/), 2026; 17 included studies.
- [*Feeds, feelings, and focus: a systematic review and meta-analysis*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41231585/), 71 studies (DOI 10.1037/bul0000498).

### Video counterpoint: play is not automatically healthier than scrolling

The user's reference video is [Tj Power, *Neuroscientist Explains: Replace Doomscrolling With Video Games*](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IfgSsgpjAnQ), **12 October 2026** (14 minutes 15 seconds). The argument, checked against English auto-captions, favours effortful, purposeful, bounded gaming, creativity and moderation over passive scrolling. **It does not endorse every game:** Power criticises rapid-reward, novelty-heavy mobile games and potentially compulsive designs.

This puts the original fast microgame idea under productive tension. *Office Attack* cannot infer that its rapid-fire play is healthier or that dopamine neatly divides into 'fast' and 'slow' biological categories. Instead it makes a smaller, defensible claim: play can be **active**, have clear success/failure consequences and **end**, while the player decides whether to pause or auto-continue between batches. This video is **popular science commentary, not experimental proof**. The research panel links to it alongside peer-reviewed evidence; claims about health are carefully attributed.

**Design response:** Relaxed breaks default ON; Marathon Mode is a separately selected autoplay-next toggle. Research reading suspends countdown/automatic advance. A finite graded ending is unavoidable. No gacha, paid bonuses, endless scrolling or forced engagement.

### 4. How the user and marker experience the research
- Each chapter ends with a celebratory 3.55 or 4.15 second animation showing score, remaining lives and the upcoming speed.
- After the celebration the animation may keep dancing. **Relaxed mode (default)** waits indefinitely for Continue, with no game timer or hazard movement. The optional **Marathon** toggle advances automatically after a short post-celebration beat, but never plays the actual microgames.
- The optional **WHY MICROGAMES?** button opens an accessible, three-tab panel containing the concise history, portability and media-analysis arguments, each with links to original publications.
- A visible **BACK TO BREAK** returns to the score/lives celebration. Pressing Escape exits the research without resuming gameplay. In relaxed mode **CONTINUE** starts the next batch after the celebration. In Marathon Mode it is optional and the next batch starts automatically, unless research was opened in that break. Opening research holds the whole break and requires a manual Continue after the reading session.
- The research is not shown between every 3-second challenge; play itself communicates the mechanical analysis.

### 5. Criticism and evaluation
The key tension: Microgames use rapid feedback and escalating pressure, which can feel joyful or exhausting. The deliberate, indefinitely extendable intermission is a design response to that conflict, not evidence that a specific break length has clinical benefits. If the player wants to read, the game makes room; if they don't, one click continues.

The actual game must be assessed on: can a first-time player interpret the verbs; do misses feel fair; are deaths comically legible; is the break satisfying; can the marker inspect sources without accidental time loss; does the player still feel ownership over their session? Browser tests establish functionality, not human satisfaction. A cold observer playtest remains a separate high-value evaluator.

- **Critical gaming culture:** the research panel ties genre, handheld/mobile contexts and short-video debate to specific design responses, with epistemic limitations explicit.
- **Inventiveness:** finite office satire, score/lives celebration, animated intermission, interactive research tabs and responsive audiovisual reactions.
